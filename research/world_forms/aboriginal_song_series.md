# Aboriginal Australian song series

## 0. A note before the rest

Songlines and the song series attached to them carry knowledge that is restricted — held by particular people, particular kin relations, and particular places, and in many cases not for public circulation. What follows is drawn only from published ethnomusicology and stays at the level of musical structure. It does not attempt to describe any songline's content, route, or ownership, and the absence of that here is deliberate rather than a gap to be filled later.

"Aboriginal Australian music" is also not one tradition. The material below concerns one documented Central Australian repertoire and one northern instrument, and generalising from either would be wrong.

## 1. Tuning

Not surveyed. Melodies are described as using narrow ranges with characteristic glides and ornaments.

## 2. Instrumentation

**Clapsticks** (bilma) hold a steady cyclical pulse, often duple, with small variations. In northern regions — Arnhem Land, where the Yolŋu name is **yidaki** — a termite-hollowed eucalyptus tube one to two metres long is played with circular breathing, sustaining a drone in the region of 50–100 Hz ([The Sound Atlas, *Indigenous Australian Music*](https://thesoundatlas.org/discover/indigenous-australian-music)).

The instrument is regional, not national. Much Central Australian singing has no didjeridu in it at all.

Circular breathing puts this alongside [`inuit_katajjaq.md`](inuit_katajjaq.md) §2, where sound is produced on both inhale and exhale: two traditions in which the breath is a continuous engine rather than a limit on phrase length, which is the opposite arrangement from [`shakuhachi_honkyoku.md`](shakuhachi_honkyoku.md) §3.

## 3. Performance structure

The documented finding worth carrying comes from the **Akwelye** repertoire of the Kaytetye-speaking people of Central Australia (Turpin, *Artfully hidden: Text and rhythm in a central Australian aboriginal song series*, [via Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/5207708/Artfully_hidden_Text_and_rhythm_in_a_central_Australian_aboriginal_song_series)):

- Every song in a song series shares **the same melodic structure**.
- Each song item has its own text, **set to an unchanging rhythm**.
- The text is **repeated until the melodic structure concludes**.
- Text lines pair as A and B, most commonly repeating as AABB.
- The same text-rhythm can be sung against different melodic contours.

A fixed rhythmic setting of a text, cycling independently against a melodic structure of its own length, until the two conclude together. That is talea and color — see [`medieval_polyphony.md`](medieval_polyphony.md) §3 — reached with no contact between the traditions, and the paper's title names the audible consequence: because the text cycle and the melodic cycle do not align, the words are *artfully hidden* rather than delivered plainly.

Two traditions, one on each side of the world, using coprime rhythmic and melodic cycles. In the French motet the effect was structural ingenuity; here obscuring the text appears to be the point.
