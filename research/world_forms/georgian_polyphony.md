# Georgian polyphony

## 1. Tuning

Non-tempered. Many Georgian songs operate outside equal temperament on intervallic relationships described as unstable or unfamiliar to classically trained singers, and some Svan singers are reported to retain the older non-tempered tuning ([The Sound Atlas](https://thesoundatlas.org/discover/georgian-polyphonic-singing); [International Research Center for Traditional Polyphony](https://polyphony.ge/en/georgia/georgian-traditional-music/forms/)).

**Confidence: low on specifics.** Every accessible source asserts non-tempered tuning; none read here gives cents values. A tradition-specific pass would need the Tbilisi research centre's own publications.

## 2. Instrumentation

Voices. Three parts: **mtkmeli** (second tenor), **modzakhili** (first tenor), and **bani** (the bass, often sung by a group rather than one voice). **Krimanchuli** is a yodelled upper-voice ostinato with register breaks; **gamqivani** a related upper part with less ostinato character.

## 3. Performance structure

The harmonic finding is the one worth carrying: **seconds, fourths, sevenths and ninths function as stable sonorities**, not as dissonances requiring resolution. The "Georgian triad" C–F–G — a fourth and a second above the bass — is characteristic and is a point of rest.

This inverts the consonance hierarchy that `core/music.js`'s `chordTones` and `PROGRESSIONS` encode. A generator built to resolve seconds outward cannot produce this music; the second *is* the goal chord. Recorded on the UNESCO Representative List ([Georgian polyphonic singing](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/georgian-polyphonic-singing-00008)).
