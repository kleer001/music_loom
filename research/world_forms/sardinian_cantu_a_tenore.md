# Sardinian cantu a tenore

## 1. Tuning

Not surveyed in cents. The four voices tune to each other for maximal fusion rather than to a standard — see §3, where fusion is the explicit goal rather than a by-product.

## 2. Instrumentation

Four male voices in a close circle, in descending pitch order: **boche** (the solo voice, carrying the text), **mesu boche**, **contra**, and **bassu** ([Cantu a tenore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantu_a_tenore); [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/canto-a-tenore-sardinian-pastoral-songs-00165)).

**The bassu engages the false vocal folds**, whose vibration produces a **sub-octave** below the pitch the true folds are producing — period doubling as a vocal technique. The contra shares the guttural timbre, produced through deliberate use of oral and nasal cavity resonance ([mdw Research Centre for European Multipart Music](https://www.mdw.ac.at/ive/emm/?PageId=155&newlang=en); [a-tenore.org](https://a-tenore.org/en/canto_a_tenore)).

## 3. Performance structure

The three lower voices form a chordal ostinato under the solo boche.

The finding worth carrying is the **quintina** — the "fifth voice". The four singers actively seek maximal blend, and when they achieve it a fifth pitch is heard that nobody is singing: an emergent combination tone from the merged spectra. The goal of the ensemble technique is to produce a voice that has no singer.

In the Gallura region a fifth *actual* voice, **tripli**, is sometimes added, doubling the fundamental an octave up at cadences — not to be confused with the quintina.

For a synthesis engine this is the inverse of the usual arrangement problem. Rather than assigning parts to produce a chord, the parts are tuned and blended so that a *sum* becomes audible — which is a statement about spectral alignment and phase coherence between voices, not about voice-leading.
