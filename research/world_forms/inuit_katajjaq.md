# Inuit katajjaq (vocal games)

## 1. Tuning

Not a pitch-set tradition. Each motif contains a low sound and a high sound; what matters is the alternation, not the interval.

## 2. Instrumentation

Two voices, traditionally two women, face to face at close range, unaccompanied. Sound is produced on **both inhale and exhale** — short, sharp rhythmic breaths in and out — so the voice never stops for air ([Inuit throat singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_throat_singing); [The Canadian Encyclopedia, *Inuit Vocal Games*](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/inuit-vocal-games-emc)).

The breath is the rhythmic engine rather than a constraint on it, which inverts [`shakuhachi_honkyoku.md`](shakuhachi_honkyoku.md) §3, where the breath sets phrase length by limiting it.

## 3. Performance structure

The structure is a canon at a very short delay, and its result is perceptual.

**The second voice's motif is identical to the first's**, entering after a time lag corresponding to the inhale/exhale alternation — about **half a beat**. Each motif is a low sound followed by a high sound. Because the two voices are offset by half a beat, a listener does not hear two motifs: they hear **a chain of low sounds and a chain of high sounds**, two streams that neither singer is performing.

That is auditory stream segregation used compositionally. The parts are identical and the offset does all the work; the emergent streams are the piece. Compare [`sardinian_cantu_a_tenore.md`](sardinian_cantu_a_tenore.md) §3, where the emergent voice is spectral rather than rhythmic, and [`central_african_hocket.md`](central_african_hocket.md), where the emergent melody comes from distributing pitches rather than delaying a copy.

It is also a game. Two singers hold the interlock until one laughs, stops, or runs out of breath — so the form's end condition is a performer failing, not a written length.
