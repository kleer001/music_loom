## 3. Performance structure

A **mensuration canon** — also prolation canon or proportional canon — has voices singing **the same melody at different speeds** simultaneously ([Prolation canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolation_canon)).

The mechanism is notational. Mensural notation leaves the duration of a note symbol dependent on the prevailing *mensuration* — the combination of tempus and prolatio in force. Put a different mensuration sign in front of the same written line and the same symbols yield different durations.

Ockeghem's **Missa prolationum** (c. 1450) is the extreme case: **notated for two voices and sung in four**. The extra parts are not written down. Performers read the same notated lines under a different mensuration, and the additional voices are derived in performance. The Kyrie I is a double prolation canon, two pairs of voices on different motifs, each pair at a **3:2** tempo ratio ([Missa prolationum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missa_prolationum)).

For a generative instrument this is about as direct as a historical technique gets: **one stored sequence, N playback rates, sounding together.** The counterpoint is a consequence of the ratios rather than of separate composition, and a rational ratio guarantees the parts realign on a predictable cycle. It is the same family of idea as [`medieval_polyphony.md`](medieval_polyphony.md) §3's talea and color — one specification, several readings, phase doing the composing.

The **ars subtilior** of the late 14th century pushes the notation further, with unusual mensuration signs and rhythmic complexity that is often read as complexity pursued for itself. The interpretation of those signs is contested and was not investigated here.

The technique survives in simplified form as augmentation and diminution canon, which is where Bach picks it up in the Goldberg canons.
