# Central African horn and flute hocket (Banda-Linda)

## 1. Tuning

Not surveyed.

## 2. Instrumentation

Wind ensembles built from tree roots, bamboo and antelope horn, alongside flutes, whistles, drums and voices. The relevant fact is not the material: **each instrument in the horn ensemble produces exactly one pitch**, and ensembles run from five to eighteen players ([Banda Linda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Linda); Arom, *African Polyphony and Polyrhythm*, ch. 4 "Polyrhythmics as a way to polyphony: Hocket", [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/african-polyphony-and-polyrhythm/polyrhythmics-as-a-way-to-polyphony-hocket/B5695A612A5D11FC8A97DD78D9286081)).

Among the wider Banda group, this use of hocket appears specific to the Dakpa and Linda peoples.

## 3. Performance structure

The melody has no player. It exists only as the sum of one-note parts entering in a contrapuntal, rhythmically organised interlock. This is the limiting case of the device that appears as imbal in [`gamelan.md`](gamelan.md) §3, ira/arca in [`andean_siku.md`](andean_siku.md) §3, and hocket in [`medieval_polyphony.md`](medieval_polyphony.md) §3 — here taken to one pitch per voice.

Structurally that is a straightforward thing to build and an unusual way to think: instead of a voice that plays a melody, N voices each holding a fixed pitch and a rhythmic slot, with the melody as an emergent property of the assignment.

Simha Arom's field recordings from 1963 onward are the basis of the documentation; the Smithsonian Folkways *Central African Republic: Banda Polyphony* release is the accessible listening source. **The Cambridge chapter was not read** — it is behind a paywall, and it is where the transcriptions and the analysis actually are.
