# World and historical musical forms

Most of what a synthesiser assumes about music is a local convention wearing a lab coat.

Twelve equal semitones, a beat that divides evenly, a melody that belongs to one player, a piece that has a beginning and an end — every one of those is a choice, and every tradition here makes a different one. Gamelan breaks the pitch table. Raga breaks the note. The maqam family breaks the semitone. Aksak breaks the even beat. West African timelines break the grid. Medieval polyphony breaks the idea that one cycle governs a piece. Tuvan overtone singing breaks the idea that the melody lives in the source at all. Steel pan breaks the assumption that an instrument's partials are given rather than chosen.

Each tradition is read the same way — how pitch is specified, what makes the sound, how a piece unfolds and who plays what when — and read for what it actually specifies rather than for atmosphere. What comes out is less a set of instruments to build than a map of where the studio's own assumptions are load-bearing, and what it would take to set each one down.

Some of the answers turn out to be cheap. Density levels that change how many notes fill a fixed cycle, one melodic line played at several speeds at once, two coprime cycles that drift against each other for bars before realigning — these are small changes to a sequencer that nothing here can currently express.

`SURVEY.md` has the method, the sources, the convergences that showed up across unrelated traditions, and an honest account of where accessible sources ran out.
