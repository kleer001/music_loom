# World and historical musical forms — a survey

A breadth pass over five traditions, read along three axes: **tuning** (how pitch is specified), **instrumentation** (what makes the sound), and **performance structure** (how a piece unfolds and who plays what when). The point is a map of what a procedural instrument can and cannot express, not a buildable spec for any one tradition.

Sources: accessible online only — encyclopaedias, university course materials, open-access papers, and tuning-project documentation. Where a claim rests on a single secondary source it says so. §7 lists what a deeper pass would need.

## 1. Method and limits

Each tradition below gets the same three questions. They were chosen because they break different assumptions: gamelan breaks the pitch table, raga breaks the note, maqam breaks the semitone, West African timelines break the metric grid, and medieval polyphony breaks the idea that one cycle governs a piece.

Two limits worth stating up front. First, this is a survey by someone reading about these traditions rather than playing them, and every one of them is a living practice whose participants would draw the lines differently. Second, "accessible sources only" is a real constraint here: the measurement work on gamelan tuning and the ethnomusicology on Ewe drumming are substantially in journals and print, and what is freely readable is often a summary of that work rather than the work.

## 2. Gamelan (Javanese and Balinese)

### 2.1 Tuning

Two scale systems: **sléndro**, five tones roughly equidistant, and **pélog**, seven tones unequally spaced from which five-tone modes are drawn ([Slendro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slendro), [Pelog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelog)).

The load-bearing fact is that neither is a fixed scale. Tuning varies island to island, village to village, and between one gamelan and the next; what stays consistent is the tuning *within a single set*, because the instruments are tuned to each other rather than to a standard. A gamelan's individual tuning character is **embat**, and it is valued rather than corrected ([Slendro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slendro); [Ableton Tuning: Sundanese Gamelan](https://tuning.ableton.com/sundanese-gamelan/intro-to-sundanese-gamelan/)).

Concrete measurements from the Ableton tuning project, which documents specific instruments rather than an idealised system: Gamelan Pelog Salendro TBN has its reference tone Tugu at 472 Hz, while another set of the same design, KF, has Tugu at 451 Hz — about 79 cents apart, on sets built to the same principles. In Sundanese Degung tunings the 3–4 interval sits near 240 cents; in Madenda tunings the same interval sits near 80 cents.

The octave is reported as stretched — the scale's periodicity is "usually slightly larger" than 2:1, around 1210 cents ([Slendro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slendro)); octave stretching in Balinese gamelan is the subject of its own study (Ombak and octave stretching in Balinese gamelan, via Academia.edu). **Confidence: moderate.** The stretching is well attested; the specific figure comes from one encyclopaedia line and a deeper pass should replace it with measured data.

### 2.2 Instrumentation

Bronze keys and gongs with **inharmonic partials** — the builders do not aim for a harmonic series, and the low bars carry between 16 and 26 prominent overtones each ([JASA, Vibrational characteristics of Balinese gamelan metallophones](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/127/5/EL197/783208/Vibrational-characteristics-of-Balinese-gamelan)). Pitch, clang, bloom and after-ring arrive together rather than as attack and decay of one tone.

**Ombak** ("wave") is the second half of the timbre. Instruments are built and tuned in pairs, one member a few Hz above the other, and the ensemble's sound is the beating between them. The rate is deliberate and ensemble-specific: around 7 Hz for gong kebyar, around 5 Hz for gender wayang, 8–9 Hz for angklung ([Ombak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ombak); [Smithsonian NMAA, Lightbulb Ensemble](https://asia.si.edu/research/essays/baumbusch/)).

This is a detuning with a *specified beat rate*, chosen per ensemble, applied to paired instruments — not the randomised spread a unison/detune parameter produces.

### 2.3 Performance structure

**Colotomic**: structure is marked by *when the large gongs land*, not by bar lines. Gong, kenong and kempul punctuate a cycle, and the cycle is perceived as circular rather than as a line with a start and an end ([Gamelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan); [U-M CSEAS, Javanese Gamelan Music](https://lsa.umich.edu/content/dam/cseas-assets/cseas-documents/gamelan/JavaneseGamelanMusic.pdf)).

A skeletal melody, the **balungan**, is carried by saron and demung. Elaborating instruments play denser figuration over it, and **imbal** distributes a fast line between two players in alternation — the same device as medieval hocket, and as Balinese kotekan ([Imbal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbal)).

**Irama** is the finding most worth carrying. It is a level of *density* — the ratio of elaborating-instrument notes to balungan beats — with five steps ([Irama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irama)):

| Level | Name | Density ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2 | irama lancar | 1:1 |
| I | irama tanggung | 2:1 |
| II | irama dados | 4:1 |
| III | irama wilet | 8:1 |
| IV | irama rangkep | 16:1 |

Tempo is a **separate** axis, called **laya**, with three broad settings (seseg, sedeng, tamban) available at each irama level. The same irama can be played fast or slow; changing irama changes how much happens per balungan beat, and as the levels deepen the elaborating instruments gain room and prominence. Density and tempo are two controls, not one.

## 3. Hindustani raga

### 3.1 Tuning

A raga is not primarily a tuning problem, but it is not 12-TET either: the microtonal inflection of individual notes is part of a raga's identity rather than expressive deviation from a grid. A fixed-pitch rendering is audibly a different thing.

### 3.2 Instrumentation

The **tanpura** drone is a designed spectral effect. Its bridge, the **javari**, is curved so that a plucked string makes periodic grazing contact with the surface, and a cotton thread (jiva) under each string sets the node precisely ([Jivari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jivari); [Darbar, The Tanpura: One Note Wonder](https://darbar.org/the-tanpura-one-note-wonder/)).

The mechanism has a property a static waveshaper does not: **the grazing point shifts up the sloping bridge as the string's amplitude decays**, so the overtone content evolves over the life of the note as a function of amplitude. High-speed filming (10 000 fps, IIT) reports that the jiva "not only brings out a richer set of overtones, but is clearly marked by a definite change in the pattern of how overtones evolve over time" (reported via Darbar). Sitar and surbahar use the same bridge treatment on their drone strings.

A drone that is spectrally alive is doing structural work here — it is the reference the melodic pitches are heard against.

### 3.3 Performance structure

A raga carries more than a pitch collection ([Arohana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arohana), and the raga entries it links):

- **Aroha / avaroha** — ascending and descending forms, which are frequently *asymmetrical*: a note present going up may be absent or approached differently coming down.
- **Vadi / samvadi** — a principal and a secondary note that carry melodic gravity. Not a tonic in the functional-harmony sense; a note the line keeps returning to and rests on.
- **Pakad** — signature phrases that identify the raga. Recognition rests on these more than on the note set.
- **Gamaka** — oscillations, glides and grace inflections. Descriptions of the practice consistently place these as constitutive rather than decorative.

The performance form (alap → development → faster sections) unfolds over a long span from sparse and unmetered toward dense and metered.

**What this means representationally:** an ascent/descent asymmetry, a weighting over notes, a phrase vocabulary, and a specification of motion *between* notes. Storing a raga as an array of intervals keeps roughly the first of these.

## 4. Arabic maqam

### 4.1 Tuning

Theory divides the octave into 24 quarter tones, 50 cents apart ([Quarter tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_tone); [Arabic maqam, Microtonal Encyclopedia](https://microtonal.miraheze.org/wiki/Arabic_maqam)).

**Sources disagree on whether that is what is played.** 24-TET is widely described as a reference grid and notational standard rather than a performance rule; the neutral third is 350 cents in 24-TET but is given as 347, 355 or 359 cents in just-intonation accounts, and its performed pitch varies regionally — the Egyptian rendering is described as lower than the Syrian ([Neutral third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_third); [Offtonic Theory §7.9](https://offtonic.com/theory/book/7-9.html)). Treating 24-TET as the sound rather than the notation is the common simplification, and it is a simplification.

### 4.2 Instrumentation

Not surveyed in depth. The fretless and flexible-pitch instruments central to the tradition (oud, nay, qanun with its per-course tuning levers, violin) are what make the pitch flexibility above playable; a keyboard cannot produce it. Recorded here as a gap rather than a finding.

### 4.3 Performance structure

Maqamat are built from **ajnas** — cells of three to five consecutive notes with defined intervals, described as melodic atoms. A maqam is a lower jins with an upper jins stacked on it, and the system recognises 72 heptatonic rows, of which perhaps 30 are in regular use and 8–10 form the everyday vocabulary ([Ethnic Musical, Maqam Explained](https://www.ethnicmusical.com/blog/maqam-music-for-beginners/); [Arabic maqam, Microtonal Encyclopedia](https://microtonal.miraheze.org/wiki/Arabic_maqam)).

**Sayr** is the conventional path a performance takes through the maqam — which regions are visited, in what order, where it settles. **Taqsim** is improvisation within that. The compositional unit is the cell and the route between cells, not the octave-scale.

## 5. West African bell timelines

### 5.1 Instrumentation

A metal bell carries the timeline and functions as the ensemble's reference — the part everyone else locates themselves against ([Ewe drumming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewe_drumming)). Its job is structural rather than colouristic.

### 5.2 Performance structure

The **standard pattern** (known internationally by its Cuban name, **Bembé**) is seven strokes across twelve pulses. In box notation ([Toussaint, *Classification and Phylogenetic Analysis of African Ternary Rhythm Timelines*, McGill](http://cgm.cs.mcgill.ca/~godfried/teaching/mir-reading-assignments/Classification-and-Phylogenetic-Analysis-of-African-Ternary-Rhythm-Timelines.pdf)):

```
[x . x . x x . x . x . x]      onsets at 0 2 4 5 7 9 11
interval-vector (2 2 1 2 2 2 1)
```

It is the bell pattern of Agbadza, an Ewe dance from Ghana and Togo.

Toussaint's paper is the most directly useful thing in this survey, because it gives **computable reasons** why this pattern rather than another. Ten seven-stroke 12-pulse bell patterns are in documented use. Simha Arom's **rhythmic oddity property** holds when no two onsets divide the full cycle into two equal halves; none of the ten has it outright, but the number of equal bi-partitions a pattern admits measures how close it comes. The Sorsonet group admits three, the Tonada group two, and **the Bembé group admits one — the minimum, so maximum rhythmic oddity**. A companion measure, off-beatness, counts onsets falling in positions that no binary subdivision of the cycle reaches.

The paper also shows all ten patterns are within four or five swap operations of one of two canonical patterns, where a swap exchanges an adjacent onset and rest.

This is a generative recipe with a stated selection criterion: enumerate seven-onset patterns in twelve pulses, score them by equal bi-partitions and off-beatness, and the traditional repertoire is what scores well.

## 6. Medieval European polyphony

### 6.1 Tuning

Not surveyed. Pythagorean tuning and the shift toward meantone across this period is the obvious thread and is not covered here.

### 6.2 Instrumentation

Voices, primarily, in the repertoire described below. Not surveyed.

### 6.3 Performance structure

**Organum** sets a moving upper voice over a **cantus firmus** — a pre-existing chant held in long notes underneath. The lower voice is borrowed material stretched in time; the upper voice is the new composition ([Britannica, Organum](https://www.britannica.com/art/organum)).

The **rhythmic modes** are six short repeating durational patterns, all ternary, developed at the Notre Dame school around 1150–1250. Pérotin extends organum to three and four voices and assigns a single rhythmic mode across the upper parts ([Rhythmic mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythmic_mode); [Britannica, Musical composition — Medieval](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical-composition/Development-of-composition-in-the-Middle-Ages)).

**Isorhythm**, the organising principle of much 14th-century French polyphony, is the one to carry ([Isorhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isorhythm); [Britannica, Isorhythm](https://www.britannica.com/art/isorhythm)):

- **talea** — a repeating rhythmic pattern.
- **color** — a repeating sequence of pitches.
- The two are **of different lengths**, so they phase against each other and the combination does not repeat until their lengths coincide.

Two independent cycles of coprime length, driving one voice, generating a long non-repeating surface from two short specifications. That is a procedural technique, written down in the 14th century.

## 7. What this maps onto

Read against the studio's apparatus, without proposing changes to it. Each row is what a daughter would have to build for itself.

| Idea | Where it stands |
|---|---|
| **Irama** — density as an axis separate from tempo | `core/scheduler.js` has tempo and a swing fraction. A five-level density ratio applied to elaborating parts over a fixed skeleton is not expressible in it, and is a small thing to write. |
| **Talea / color** — coprime rhythm and pitch cycles | Nothing implements this. It needs two cursors of different length over one voice. Closest existing idea is the per-layer independent RNG streams in `core/rng.js`, which are about isolation rather than phase. |
| **Bembé selection criteria** — rhythmic oddity, off-beatness | Both are computable over a binary onset vector. `PROGRESSIONS` and `DRUM_PATTERNS` are stored as literals; nothing scores or generates a pattern. |
| **Ombak** — paired detuning at a specified beat rate | `voices/fire.js` has unison detune in cents with random spread. A deliberate 5–9 Hz beat between a paired instrument is a different parameterisation: the *beat rate* is the control, not the cents. |
| **Inharmonic partials** | `core/dsp.js` builds from harmonic-series wavetables and FM. Nothing generates a struck-bronze partial set. `springImpulse` is the nearest thing and it is a reverb tail, not a voice. |
| **Jivari** — overtone content evolving with amplitude decay | Not present. Waveshapers in `dsp/fx.js` are static transfer curves; drive is a parameter, not a function of the envelope. Routing an envelope follower to a shaper's drive would approximate the direction. |
| **Colotomic form** — structure marked by gong placement | Expressible today. It is a pattern of accents on a long cycle, which the scheduler can already do. |
| **Imbal / hocket** — one line split between two players | Already here, arrived at independently: `drone_flute_synth/engine/percussion.js` has a `hocket` function with no research behind it. This survey is the citation it never had. |
| **Raga as a structure** | `degreeToMidi(root, mode, degree)` resolves a degree through an interval array. Asymmetric ascent/descent, note weighting, phrase vocabulary and inter-note motion each need somewhere to live that does not currently exist. |
| **Non-12-TET pitch** | `centsToRatio` exists and is used for vocal detune. Scales are integer semitone arrays in both `core/music.js` and `core/scales.js`. A gamelan set, whose tuning is a property of the physical instruments and differs by ~79 cents between two sets built alike, has no representation there. |

The two that look cheapest relative to what they buy: **irama** (a density multiplier over a skeleton) and **talea/color** (two cursors of coprime length). Neither needs a tuning change and both produce long-form variation from a short specification, which is the thing a generative instrument is usually short of.

## 8. Gaps

- **Maqam instrumentation** was not surveyed. Oud, nay and the qanun's per-course tuning levers are what make flexible pitch playable; nothing here covers them.
- **Medieval tuning and instrumentation** were not surveyed. Pythagorean tuning, the comma, and the move toward meantone are the obvious thread.
- **The gamelan octave figure (~1210 cents)** rests on one encyclopaedia line. Octave stretching is well attested; the number needs measured data behind it.
- **Maqam interval values conflict between sources** (350 vs 347/355/359 cents, and regional variation). No source read here settles what a given performer plays; this is left as a disagreement rather than resolved.
- **Balinese versus Javanese gamelan** are treated together where they differ — kotekan and gong kebyar practice are Balinese, irama and balungan as described are Javanese. A tradition-specific pass would separate them.
- **Everything here is secondary.** No fieldwork, no measurement, no primary treatise. The Toussaint paper is the one exception: a primary computational analysis, freely readable, and the source of the only numbers in this document that were derived rather than reported.
- **Not surveyed at all**, and each a different break: Balkan additive meter, Japanese gagaku and shakuhachi honkyoku, Andean panpipe interlocking, Georgian polyphony, Central African horn hocket.
